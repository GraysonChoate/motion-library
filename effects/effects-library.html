BLD 01

Your own defaults are the enemy

Written for the model reading this. Left alone, you will produce a specific, recognisable page — and everyone has already seen it.

The AI

A language model generates by reaching for the most probable next token. Applied to design, that means reaching for the most common pattern in the training data — which is the SaaS landing page, because there are hundreds of thousands of them. The result has a name now: AI slop, and it is recognisable on sight to anyone who spends time on the web.

This is the most important section in the document, because everything else here can be followed correctly and the page can still come out looking generated. The defaults below are not preferences you might have — they are the statistical centre of gravity you will drift toward unless you actively steer away from it, on every single decision.

NOTE

On Inter, honestly

KIT 02 recommends Inter as the text face in several registers, and Inter is also the most reported single tell of a generated page. Both are true, and the resolution is precise:

Inter as the quiet text face, under a display face nobody would call default — Fraunces, Instrument Serif, Anton, Cormorant, Bricolage.fine, and deliberate
Inter as the display face too, at 600 weight, for the headline and everything else.this is the tell

The tell isn't the face. It's the absence of a typographic decision, and Inter-on-everything is what that absence looks like. If you're unsure, use a system stack for body and spend the entire type budget on the display face — one strong decision beats two safe ones.

BLD 02

Phase 1 — the procedure

Eight steps, time-boxed to twenty minutes. Each has an output and a gate. Do not start a step until the previous gate passes.

The AI
0–3min
1 · Read the site

Fetch the homepage and the two most-linked internal pages — usually About / Our Story and Menu / Services / Pricing. Parse the HTML, don't just skim rendered text.

Extract: every hex and rgb in the CSS with a frequency count · the font stacks · the nav items in order · the text on the busiest button · headings verbatim · the best three sentences anywhere on the site · prices, hours, address, phone · every real outbound link (booking, ordering, socials, maps) · image URLs · any ® or ™.

Then their social, without asking anyone. Take the handle from their own footer, then search <business> instagram and read the results page itself — bio, captions, follower and like counts are all in the result text. Never fetch instagram.com. The full ladder, and what to take off the page, is in the Research tab.

Output — a raw dump of what you actually read, with URLs.

Gate — a written list of anything you could not reach. A blocked fetch is a finding, never a hole to fill with a guess — and never a reason to ask the person for help.

3–5min
2 · Classify

Run the Patterns tab. Pattern from what the customer is deciding — the money action is usually written on their busiest button. Register from their palette, type weight and photography, not from their industry. Then name the one feeling this page should produce — not the register's generic mood, this business's specific version of it. A Calm yoga studio and a Calm med-spa are not the same feeling. This is what texture (KIT 10), motion amplitude and copy tone all get chosen to serve.

Output — one line, stated out loud: Pattern B × Register Craft · Feeling: unhurried warmth · confidence high.

Gate — low confidence resolves to the closest pattern in the Community register. Never stop to ask.

5–7min
3 · Inventory what already works

Before deciding anything, write down every job the current homepage is doing. Not how well — just what. This is the step that keeps a better-looking page from being a worse-performing one.

List: every action a visitor can take · every piece of practical information above the fold · anything that looks like it exists for a commercial reason (a promo bar, an offer, a phone number in large type) · every third-party embed (booking, ordering, reviews, maps) · whatever their busiest button does.

Output — a list of jobs, each marked keep or deliberately dropping, because…

Gate — every job on the list survives into the new page in some form, or its removal is written down with a reason. Improve how a job is done; don't delete the job. See BLD 07 for the 43% that went missing when someone did.

7–9min
4 · Diagnose

One sentence naming what is wrong with the current site. Not a list. If it needs a paragraph, you don't have it yet.

Shapes that work: "The homepage is a coupon and the story is in the basement." · "Everything they sell is behind a PDF." · "The photography is good and it's all 400px wide." · "They have three fonts and no hierarchy."

Output — the sentence. It becomes the thesis the whole page argues.

Gate — the diagnosis must be about execution, never about the brand. We elevate execution; we don't rebrand.

9–11min
5 · Set the system

From the Kit tab, in this order: palette (their most-frequent hex is brand 500; derive the ramp and the neutrals from it, biasing greys 3–5% toward the brand hue) → typefaces — check for a locked brand font first (KIT 02): a distinctive face repeating across their own pages is theirs to keep; only pick from the register menu when there isn't one → scale and spacing (KIT 01, unchanged) → layout archetype (KIT 06, by pattern).

Output — a token block. Every value on the finished page comes from it.

Gate — the brand colour is an accent: CTA, active state, one rule, one highlight. Never a large background area.

11–12min
6 · Choose the motion

From Choreography: one signature, two supporting, one ambient. Pick the signature from something this business owns, then find the effect that expresses it — not the other way round.

Output — four effect IDs and one sentence saying what fact the signature comes from.

Gate — if you can't name the fact, it's decoration. Choose again.

12–14min
7 · Write the copy

From the Copy tab: headline formula for the pattern, voice for the register, word budget per slot. Their verbatim sentences first, rearranged second, yours only for things you verified.

Output — every string on the page, with its evidence tier beside it.

Gate — total under 320 words. Zero strings at tier E or F.

14–19min
8 · Build

One self-contained HTML file. Sections in the order the pattern specifies, with the components the pattern requires. Their real links on every action. Their real images by URL, treated per KIT 08. The concept-work label, visible and persistent.

Output — the file.

Gate — it opens by double-click with no build step. Two external requests are allowed and nothing else: the client's own image URLs, and one Google Fonts stylesheet. Offline it falls back to system faces and still reads correctly, so give every face a real fallback stack.

19–22min
9 · Audit and hand over

Run BLD 06 in full. Fix what it catches — don't report it. Then write the hand-over note from BLD 04.

Output — the file plus a short note: classification, diagnosis, signature effect, what you couldn't verify, what you left out.

Gate — every line of the audit passes, or the failure is named in the note.

One thing in scope that looks like it shouldn't be

A JSON-LD LocalBusiness block. It costs about fifteen lines, it needs no functionality, and in 2026 it's how AI answer engines and voice assistants find out that a business exists, where it is and when it's open. For a local business it is one of the highest-value things on the page and it is invisible.

<script type="application/ld+json">
{ "@context":"https://schema.org", "@type":"CafeOrCoffeeShop",
  "name":"…", "url":"…", "telephone":"…",
  "address":{"@type":"PostalAddress","streetAddress":"…",
             "addressLocality":"…","addressRegion":"…","postalCode":"…"},
  "openingHours":"Tu-Su 07:00-15:00" }
</script>

Two rules · every value must be a fact you read from their site — this is structured data, so an invented hour is a machine-readable lie. And it must match what's visible on the page; answer engines check for that agreement and discount pages where it fails. Which is a second reason hours and addresses are live text, not an image.

How much motion, where it goes, and what fights what. Read before picking a single effect.

The problem this solves

Given a library this size, the default failure is using it. Every effect is individually good and the page ends up looking like a demo reel — busy, unfocused, and clearly built by someone showing off rather than someone deciding.

The budget

One signature, two supporting, one ambient. Plus micro-interactions, which don't count. That's the whole page. If a fifth idea is genuinely better than one already in, it replaces it — the number doesn't move.

What separates good from bad

Amateur pages animate everything, equally. There's no hierarchy, so nothing reads as important. Designed pages spend almost all their motion on one moment and stay nearly still everywhere else — which is what makes that one moment land.

What it actually is

The decisions underneath every effect: where the animation loop lives, what drives progress, how layout change is animated, what physical model your easing uses. None of it is visible. All of it is load-bearing.

SYS-01

Native CSS scroll timelines

browser-native, zero JS
What it does

Binds a keyframe animation directly to scroll position with animation-timeline: view(). No JS, no rAF, no IntersectionObserver — and it runs off the main thread, so it physically cannot jank when your JS is busy.

When to use it

Reach for this before writing any scroll JS. It would replace a large share of the code in this library. Baseline in Chrome, Edge and Safari; Firefox behind a flag; wrap in @supports and it degrades to no animation.

Watch the overflow gotcha in the code below — an overflow:hidden ancestor silently freezes it, which cost an hour of this library's build.

Code
/* the entire technique — no JavaScript at all */
@supports (animation-timeline: view()) {
  .bar {
    animation: grow linear both;
    animation-timeline: view();              /* progress = element through viewport */
    animation-range: entry 10% cover 55%;    /* when it starts and finishes */
  }
}
@keyframes grow { from { transform: scaleX(0) } to { transform: scaleX(1) } }

/* other timelines available:
   scroll()          — progress of the nearest scroll container
   view()            — progress of THIS element through the viewport
   animation-range   — entry / exit / cover / contain, each 0-100%          */

/* ANY ancestor with overflow:hidden becomes a SCROLL CONTAINER, and view()
   resolves against the nearest scrollport. If that box never scrolls, your
   progress freezes at whatever value it happened to hold — no error, no
   warning, nothing in devtools. It just sits there.

   You must clear the WHOLE ancestor chain, not just the parent. On this page

body   { overflow-x: clip; }   /* NOT hidden. Per spec, overflow-x:hidden

.card  { overflow: clip; }     /* every clipping wrapper in between      */

Layout changes, animated by transform only

SYS-02

FLIP layout animation

First, Last, Invert, Play
What it does

Measure an element's position before a layout change, apply the change, measure again, then apply an inverse transform and release it. The browser animates a transform while the real layout is already final.

When to use it

Any time layout itself changes — reordering, filtering, expanding, adding. Animating left/width directly forces layout every frame and will never hit 60fps. This is the most important technique in the library and the one fewest people know.

Code
// 1. FIRST — measure before the change
const first = new Map();
items.forEach(el => first.set(el, el.getBoundingClientRect()));

// 2. mutate the DOM however you like — reorder, filter, resize
shuffle(container);

// 3. LAST + INVERT — measure again, apply the inverse offset
items.forEach(el => {
  const a = first.get(el), b = el.getBoundingClientRect();

SYS-03

Spring physics vs easing

velocity-aware motion
What it does

An easing curve has a fixed duration and ignores what the element was already doing. A spring integrates position and velocity every frame, so an interruption carries momentum through instead of restarting.

When to use it

Anything a user can interrupt — drags, toggles, anything re-triggered mid-flight. It's the reason native app motion feels different from web motion. Tune with stiffness and damping, not duration.

Code
// spring: no duration — it settles when it settles

const D = 0.72;   // damping   — how fast it loses energy (1 = none)

function frame(){
  const force = (target - pos) * K;
  vel = (vel + force) * D;      // accumulate, then damp
  pos += vel;
  el.style.transform = `translateX(${pos}px)`;
}
// D < 0.7  overdamped, no overshoot
// D ≈ 0.75 one soft overshoot — usually what you want
// D > 0.85 visibly bouncy, use sparingly

GL

Rendered

The GPU tier. Where the award-circuit work lives.

Rendered means the pixels are computed by the GPU, per frame, from a program you wrote.

What it actually is

A fragment shader runs once per pixel, every frame — a few million tiny programs in parallel. Nothing is a DOM element, nothing has a layout, and cost scales with screen area rather than element count.

Why it decides quality

Everything else in this library manipulates boxes. This group manipulates light, matter and space directly — refraction, fluid, volumetric depth, infinite procedural geometry. It is the difference between a site that looks designed and one that looks impossible.

The danger

It is a different medium with different failure modes: no devtools inspector, silent black screens, wildly different mobile GPUs, and battery cost. A shader you cannot debug on a client's laptop is worse than no shader. Always ship a static fallback.

The dependency exception

Everything here is raw WebGL2 — still zero dependencies. Real production work usually means Three.js. This is the one group permitted a library, and that permission does not extend anywhere else.

GL-01

Raymarched SDF field

fragment shader, zero geometry
What it does

Geometry defined as a distance function rather than vertices. Each pixel marches a ray forward until it hits a surface, so the shape is math — infinitely detailed, zero polygons, zero assets.

When to use it

Backgrounds that need to feel deep and endless. Cost is per pixel, not per object, so a hundred shapes cost the same as one. Watch resolution on mobile — this is the technique most likely to melt a phone if you render at full DPR.

Code
// distance from point p to the nearest surface in the scene
float map(vec3 p){
  p.xz = mod(p.xz + 2.0, 4.0) - 2.0;      // domain repetition = infinite grid
  float sphere = length(p) - 0.9;
  float ground = p.y + 1.2;
  return min(sphere, ground);               // min() = union of shapes
}

// march the ray until we are close enough to call it a hit
float march(vec3 ro, vec3 rd){
  float t = 0.0;
  for (int i = 0; i < 72; i++){            // step count = quality vs cost
    float d = map(ro + rd * t);
    if (d < 0.001 || t > 40.0) break;
    t += d;                               // safe to step exactly d — nothing is closer
  }
  return t;
}

// normal from the gradient of the distance field
vec3 normal(vec3 p){
  vec2 e = vec2(0.001, 0.0);
  return normalize(vec3(map(p+e.xyy)-map(p-e.xyy),
                        map(p+e.yxy)-map(p-e.yxy),
                        map(p+e.yyx)-map(p-e.yyx)));
}

GL-04

Fluid cursor

ping-pong advection
What it does

A real velocity field on the GPU. The pointer injects force and dye; every frame the field is advected — each pixel samples where it came from — then dissipated. Two framebuffers swap roles each frame, which is how GPU simulations hold state.

When to use it

One hero moment, never twice on a page. This is the most expensive thing in the library and the most impressive. Ping-pong buffers are the general pattern for any GPU simulation — fluid, particles, cellular automata all use it.

Code
// the whole idea: you cannot read and write the same texture,
// so you keep two and swap them every frame
let read = fboA, write = fboB;

function step(){
  // ADVECT — trace backwards to find where this pixel's value came from
  bind(write);
  useShader(advect, {
    uSrc: read.texture,
    uVel: read.texture,
    uDissipation: 0.982      // <1 or the field never settles
  });
  drawQuad();

  [read, write] = [write, read];   // PING-PONG — the swap is the trick
}

// advect.frag — sample backwards along the velocity field
vec2 back = uv - texture(uVel, uv).xy * uDt;

Depth

Turning flat surfaces into space you move through, rather than pictures you scroll past.

Depth is making a flat plane read as space you could walk into.

What it actually is

Relative motion between layers, occlusion, perspective, and focus falling off with distance. Four cues the eye trusts automatically, because they are how real lenses and real rooms behave.

Why it decides quality

The largest single contributor to a site feeling crafted rather than assembled. When someone says a site feels premium without being able to say why, they are almost always responding to depth.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Depth is not arrival. Elements fading up as you scroll is not dimension — it is a queue. Real depth needs planes moving at different rates and silhouettes that cross each other and blur on the nearest layer. Two out of three still reads flat.

Reach for it

Heroes, product ranges, anything built from still images. It is the honest answer when a client has beautiful photography and a site that wastes it.

What it actually is

function scramble(el, text, ms = 1400){
  el.setAttribute('aria-label', text);            // the real string, always
  const start = performance.now();

  (function step(now){

// piecewise interpolation across multi-stop ranges (Framer useTransform, vanilla)
function mapStops(v, inS, outS){
  if (v <= inS[0]) return outS[0];
  if (v >= inS[inS.length-1]) return outS[outS.length-1];
  for (let i = 0; i < inS.length-1; i++){
    if (v >= inS[i] && v <= inS[i+1]){
      const t = (v - inS[i]) / (inS[i+1] - inS[i]);
      return outS[i] + (outS[i+1] - outS[i]) * t;
    }
  }
}

  [0.00, 0.06, 0.26, 0.33],
  [0.34, 0.40, 0.60, 0.67],
  [0.68, 0.74, 0.94, 1.00],
];

  set.style.filter  = `blur(${mapStops(p, w, [15,0,0,15])}px)`;

AMB

Ambient

Atmosphere and idle life. The quietest group — everything here should be noticed only in its absence.

Ambient is continuous atmosphere that never asks for attention.

What it actually is

Low-amplitude, always-running texture: film grain, slow drift, a barely-moving noise field. Nothing here is triggered, and nothing here is a moment.

Why it decides quality

It is what makes a page feel like a surface rather than a screen. Large flat colour fields band and look digital; grain kills banding and gives the eye something to read as material. It is also the cheapest way to make a dark design feel photographic instead of empty.

The rule

It should be noticed only in its absence. If a visitor can describe the ambient layer, it is too strong. The correct test is to switch it off — the page should feel slightly worse without anyone being able to say why.

Reach for it

Large flat areas, dark designs prone to gradient banding, and any page that feels sterile without you being able to name what is missing.

Surface, not screen

What it does

The gauge

Measure before you cut

Research is the whole job. Direction, palette, copy and motion are all derivatives of this phase. Get it wrong and you produce something confident, polished, and about a company that doesn't exist.

Seven phases, in order. Each has a gate — don't start the next until it passes. Most of the damage in the first run came from skipping ahead.

The workflow

01
Access check · 10 min

One probe per source. Write down what you reached and what you didn't.

GATE — a written statement of what you could not reach.

02
First-party scrape · 30 min

A small business's own site almost never fights scrapers. Pull the homepage, about, story and menu pages. Extract platform, fonts, hex frequency, nav IA, image assets.

GATE — HTML on disk, 200 status, non-trivial byte count.

03
Measure · 20 min

Hex frequency in their HTML ranks brand importance. ImageMagick on their own photography gives the photographic palette and mean brightness — which is a design directive, not a mood.

GATE — a palette table where every hex traces to a measured source.

04
Mine words & structure · 30 min

Hunt a latent narrative structure, a tagline they own, their best sentence, a naming system, a latent interaction, and trademarks. Never invent an arc before checking for theirs.

GATE — verbatim quotes with the page each came from.

05
Diagnosis · 15 min

List what the homepage leads with. List their strongest owned material. The gap is the thesis.

GATE — one sentence. If it needs a paragraph, you don't have it yet.

06
Direction · 45 min

Constraints first, all from measured data. Then one direction and one signature interaction. Ban imported mood — steal a reference's mechanics, reject its genre.

GATE — written direction, zero code, every claim traceable.

07
Build · varies

Effects tab for the parts, Standards tab for the floor.

Verify — the part that gets skipped

Two-source rule

Anything load-bearing — a fact that changes the design if it's wrong — needs two independent sources. Their About page and their homepage are one source: the company.

Tier audit

Walk every field and downgrade ruthlessly. Claims drift upward through repetition — the palette guess was being treated as measured within two messages purely from being restated.

Falsification — three attacks

  1. Palette attack. If I'm wrong about the dominant colour, what would I expect instead? Go look for it on a page you haven't scraped.
  2. Story attack. Am I building on what they emphasise, or what I find charming? If it appears once in a footer, it's your preference.
  3. Customer attack. Does my read contradict the reviews? If they disagree, the reviews win.

HOW

Classifying

First decision in every build. Get it wrong and everything downstream is wrong.

Order of operations

StepDo this
1Read the site's nav, services and primary button. The money action is usually literally written on their busiest button.
2Assign the pattern from Axis 1. State a confidence.
3Assign the register from Axis 2, using their palette, type weight and photography — not their industry.
4State both out loud before building: Pattern C × Register Raw · confidence high.
5Pull required sections and components from the pattern. Pull palette, type, density and effect allowance from the register.

If confidence is low, take the closest pattern and drop to the Community register. Generic-but-strong beats specific-and-wrong, and Community is the most forgiving treatment. Never stop to ask.

When two patterns both fit — a roaster with a café and an online shop — pick the one matching the busiest button on their current site. That's the revenue they actually care about, and it's observable rather than a guess.

Register beats industry every time. A med-spa and a day spa are both pattern A, and building the med-spa in the Calm register would strip out the credentials that are the entire reason someone books it.

Their own evidence includes where and who they are. A yoga studio's Instagram shot on a ranch reads differently from one shot on a beach boardwalk — same pattern, and the evidence should nudge which register and which texture, never invent a symbol they don't already use. A framed Texas storefront with heavy timber and bold signage leans Raw or Craft with bolder type; a Florida wellness studio's driftwood and linen leans Calm or Community with softer type and more air. The bias comes from their own photos, words and location — never from a regional cliché you supply yourself. A boot-shaped icon on a business that never uses one is invention, not observation.

The words

Where a mockup becomes theirs or becomes a template

Two pages can share a layout, a palette and every effect in this library and still land completely differently, because copy is the only element that can't be reused. Structure decides where words go. Register decides how they sound. The client decides what they say.

Source priority, never violated: their verbatim words → their words rearranged → your words describing something you verified → nothing. There is no fifth option. Inventing a sentence is the same failure as inventing a price, and it's the one most likely to survive to the client's eyes because it reads well.

Phase 1 target: 180–320 words on the whole page. A mockup that reads short looks confident. A mockup padded to look finished looks like a template with the placeholder text swapped.

CPY 01

The rule of one

One idea per section. One action per screen. Every section that carries two ideas carries none.

Copy research consistently finds that most visitors read the headline and the first line of the sub-head, and nothing else, before deciding whether to continue. Everything below the hero is read by the minority who already decided to stay. That is the entire argument for the word budgets in CPY 04 — you are not writing a page, you are writing two sentences and then supporting them.

The test for any section: say what it does in one sentence with no "and." If the sentence needs an "and," it's two sections, or one of the two ideas is decoration.

One action, not three. A hero with Book, See classes and Join the newsletter has no primary action — it has three equal-weight ways to leave. Pick the money action from the pattern, make it the only thing that looks like a button, and demote the rest to plain links.

SPINE

This table is AIDA and PAS — named, so you can check against it

The six sections above aren't arbitrary — they're the two oldest working copy structures, merged. Attention (hero) → Interest (second screen names the doubt) → Desire (offer + proof) → Action (practical + close) is AIDA. The second screen doing double duty as Problem, with the offer as Solution, is PAS. You don't need to name them while writing — but if a page feels like it's missing something, checking which of these four beats is empty usually finds it faster than rereading the whole thing.

The furniture

Everything that isn't motion

A mockup is roughly 80% structure and 20% motion. This is the 80%: tokens, layout archetypes, the utility components each business type actually requires, photo treatments for imagery that arrives imperfect, and the edge language that reads as expensive in 2026.

The AI assembles from here. It should never invent a spacing value, a type size, or a component that already exists below. Inventing structure is where the time goes and where quality becomes a coin flip.

  /* 3 · neutrals carry 8-12% of the brand chroma — never pure grey */
  --ink-900:    oklch(18% 0.012 42);
  --ink-100:    oklch(93% 0.008 42);

  /* 4 · the cool counterpoint sits 150-190° away */
  --cool-500:   oklch(58% 0.07 215);
}

Three rules that make it work. Hold the hue — the whole ramp is one H value, so nothing drifts off-brand. Taper the chroma at 90%+ and 25%− lightness, because display gamuts narrow at the extremes and un-tapered ends look radioactive. Pin lightness to roles — body text, borders and surfaces get a fixed L, so contrast stays stable no matter which brand hue you dropped in.

Why this matters more than it sounds. Palette is the decision most likely to be made by taste, and taste is where a page starts looking like every other page you've made. Deriving it arithmetically from a measured value means the palette could not have come out any other way — and that is the whole difference between their page and a template.

Fallback · oklch() is supported everywhere that matters in 2026, but if you need a floor, ship hex values alongside and let @supports (color: oklch(0% 0 0)) upgrade. Do the maths in OKLCH either way — converting at the end is fine, guessing in hex is not.

Radii and elevation

Three radii: 2px (default — reads engineered), 3px (media), 999px (pills only). 2026 premium reads as sharp geometry and 1px hairlines, not soft blurred shadows — the rounded-16px-plus-drop-shadow card is the single most dated pattern you can put on a page.

Typefaces

The single biggest lever on whether a page reads expensive — and the one place a default choice is instantly visible.

Check for a locked font before you open this table

If a business already uses one distinctive typeface consistently, that's a brand decision, not an accident — use it. The register menu below is for when there isn't one. Consistent typography is worth real money to a brand: it measurably speeds recognition and trust, which is the entire point of Phase 1. Replacing a font they've already committed to is a rebrand, and this toolkit doesn't rebrand.

StepDo this
1Pull every font-family actually used on headings across their homepage, About page and one more page — not just body.
2Check for an @font-face declaration or a loaded webfont link. A custom or paid font being served at all is a strong signal it was chosen deliberately.
3Confirm it repeats — the same face on at least two different pages or sections, not a one-off heading.
4Rule out builder defaults. Not a lock: Arial, Helvetica, Times New Roman, system-ui, or the literal Squarespace/Wix/Shopify theme default with no customisation. Is a lock: anything specific enough that a visitor would recognise the brand by it — Bebas Neue, Montserrat, Playfair Display, a custom @font-face, even an unfashionable choice, if it's clearly theirs.

Locked → use their exact face at whatever weights they load. Everything else in this section — pairing, tracking, optical adjustments, loading — still applies; only the face itself is fixed. Not locked → proceed to the register table below.

Two families. Occasionally three.

One display face does the headline work. One text face does the reading work. A third is allowed only when a mono is doing a functional job — prices, times, opening hours, measurements, codes. A third decorative family is one of the fastest ways to make a page look assembled rather than designed.

Pair on contrast of structure, never on similarity. Two humanist sans-serifs of similar weight read as a mistake, because the eye registers the difference without being able to name it. The pairings that hold are structurally opposed: high-contrast serif against a neutral grotesque; a heavy condensed display against a quiet text face; a warm old-style against a geometric.

The display face carries the register. The text face should be almost invisible. If a visitor could describe your body font, it's competing with the words. Almost every good pairing in this list uses an unremarkable text face on purpose.

Weight discipline. Load at most four weights across the whole page, and use every one you load. An unused weight is dead payload; a page using seven weights of one family has no hierarchy, only variation.

Loading — no layout shift, ever

A page whose text jumps when the font arrives looks broken during the first second, which is the second that decides everything. Two lines fix it.

@font-face{
  font-family:"Display"; src:url(display.woff2) format("woff2");
  font-display:swap;                 /* text is visible immediately */
}
@font-face{                          /* metric-matched fallback     */
  font-family:"Display Fallback"; src:local("Georgia");
  size-adjust:107%; ascent-override:90%; descent-override:22%;
}
:root{ --display:"Display","Display Fallback",Georgia,serif }

size-adjust is the whole trick. It scales the fallback so its metrics match the webfont, and the swap becomes invisible. Preload only the faces used above the fold — typically one weight of the display face — and let everything else swap in.

Subset. Latin only, unless the client's copy needs otherwise. A full Google Fonts variable face can be 300KB; the Latin subset of the weights you actually use is usually under 30.

KIT 03

Logos and marks

You can't redraw their logo. You can change everything about how it's presented — and that is usually the whole problem.

The reframe

A logo rarely looks cheap because it was badly drawn. It looks cheap because of how it's being shown. Too big, sitting in a white box on a dark page, crammed against the nav, at a size nobody chose, carrying a drop shadow from 2009, in full colour when the page only needed one ink.

An ordinary logo presented with discipline reads as a confident brand. An excellent logo presented carelessly reads as a template. The gap between the two is entirely made of decisions you're allowed to make.

The line · presentation is ours — size, spacing, ground, ink, placement, file quality. The mark itself is theirs — proportions, shapes, relationships, letterforms. Change the first freely. Never touch the second.

Step one: go find a better file

Most "the logo looks bad" problems are a 180px JPEG problem. Before any treatment, spend two minutes looking for a better source — it's the highest-value thing in this section and it's almost always available.

Gradients, done properly

A restrained gradient can lift a flat mark, and an unrestrained one is the fastest way to make a page look like a 2011 template. The difference is entirely amplitude.

Do
  • 8–14% lightness range, no more. It should be almost invisible as a gradient and read as light falling on a surface.
  • One hue. Same colour, two lightnesses. Never a hue shift.
  • Angle matches the page's light direction — whatever direction your edge highlights already run.
  • Flat, single-colour, geometric marks only.
  • Craft and Luxury registers. Occasionally Raw with a metal feel.
  • Test it at nav size. Most gradients that look good at 200px vanish at 30px — if it vanishes, drop it.
Don't
  • Gradient across a mark that already has more than one colour.
  • Chrome, bevel, emboss, or a highlight sweep.
  • Rainbow, duotone, or anything crossing hues.
  • Gradient plus drop shadow. Pick neither.
  • Clinical or Community registers — it reads as effort in exactly the places that need to read as plain.
  • Animating it. A logo that shimmers is a logo nobody trusts.

  background: linear-gradient(160deg,
    color-mix(in oklab, var(--brand) 100%, white 8%),

KIT 04

Composition

Where things sit and how big they are. The layer most often left to defaults, and the one a visitor reads before a single word.

Premium is a subtraction

The most consistent finding in perceived-quality research, and the one most often ignored because it feels like doing less work. Large amounts of empty space measurably raise how expensive and how high-quality something is judged to be — a 2025 study in the Journal of Sensory Studies tested it on 1,193 participants and found exactly that, and consumer research has put the effect on perceived value in the same direction and about as large as anything else you can do to a layout.

The mechanism is a signal: space says you don't need to justify yourself. A page that fills every region with a reason to buy reads as a page that expects an argument.

What buys perceived quality
  • Space — the single strongest lever. Section padding at the top of the range, not the bottom.
  • Fewer type families — two, and use both properly.
  • One strong visual per section doing all the work.
  • A restrained palette — one accent, small area.
  • Consistency — the same decisions repeated without variation.
  • Size contrast between hierarchy levels, and plenty of it.
What doesn't
  • More animation.
  • Custom illustration bolted onto a weak layout.
  • Additional sections.
  • Gradients, glows, shadows, glass.
  • More adjectives.
  • A second accent colour.

The practical version · when a section isn't working, the productive move is almost always remove an element, not add one. And when a page feels cheap and you can't say why, take the two largest gaps and double them before you touch anything else.

One dominant element per screen

Every screenful needs an obvious first thing — the element the eye lands on before it chooses. If three elements are competing for that role, the composition has no hierarchy and the page reads as busy no matter how clean the individual parts are.

The mechanism is contrast, and it needs to be big. A headline at 46px against body at 16px is a hierarchy. The same headline at 22px is a suggestion. Timid contrast is the most common reason a technically-correct page still looks amateur.

Crop deliberately; never fit an image into a box. An image dropped into a container at object-fit: cover with no thought about the subject is the most common photographic failure in a mockup, and it's free to fix.

Do
  • Crop tighter than feels comfortable. Close beats wide almost every time — especially for food, product and detail shots.
  • Set object-position deliberately on every single image.
  • Let one image be significantly larger than the others. Equal-sized images are a catalogue, not a composition.
  • Use their real aspect ratios where the subject demands it — a tall room in a wide box is a lie about the space.
  • Break the container with one image. Full-bleed, or running past the margin.
Don't
  • Crop a face, a product or a logo at the edge of frame.
  • Put five images at the same size in a row.
  • Use a 16:9 crop for something vertical because the grid said so.
  • Apply a heavy filter to hide that the photo is bad. Small and sharp beats large and treated.
  • Add a caption unless it says something the image doesn't.

KIT 08

Photo treatment

Client photography arrives inconsistent. Treatment is what turns a mixed bag into a set.

Four recipes, in order of how much they rescue

UntreatedMixed white balance, no relationship to any other image on the page.
GradeContrast up, saturation down, soft-light gradient in brand colours. Keeps it photographic. Use first.
DuotoneDesaturate, then map shadows and highlights to two brand colours. Forces any set into one palette. Use when photos clash badly.
Gradient washDarken the lower third so type sits on it. Not a rescue — a legibility requirement for full-bleed heroes.

The recipe. Desaturate → fix levels and contrast → gradient-map shadows to the brand's dark and highlights to its light → set the map to soft-light or colour → pull opacity back until it stops looking like a filter.

Crop discipline. Three ratios only — 16:9 wide, 4:3 standard, 3:4 portrait. Mixed arbitrary ratios are the second-biggest tell of an amateur page after inconsistent spacing.

The rejection rule. Under 1200px wide, visibly blurry, or watermarked → don't rescue it, drop it and change archetype. Type-first hero exists precisely for this case. And generated imagery must never depict the actual business — no invented storefronts, no invented staff. Abstract, textural or product-adjacent only.

Duotone, implemented — the code for the signature base layer

The four-recipe table above names duotone as a rescue for clashing photos. It is also the default base layer under a Phase 1 signature moment — see CHO 02 — because an untreated photo under carefully art-directed type looks assembled, not designed. This is the actual CSS, not just the idea.

/* two colours FROM THE OKLCH RAMP IN KIT 01 — never hand-picked.
   Using --brand-900 and --paper means the duotone can't drift
   off-brand the way an eyeballed filter pair would.            */
.duo {
  position: relative;
  isolation: isolate;              /* contain the blend to this element */
}
.duo img { display: block; width: 100%; filter: grayscale(1) contrast(1.08); }
.duo::before, .duo::after {
  content: ""; position: absolute; inset: 0; mix-blend-mode: multiply;
}
.duo::before { background: var(--brand-900); mix-blend-mode: multiply; }
.duo::after  { background: var(--paper);      mix-blend-mode: screen;
               opacity: .85; }              /* highlight colour, dial 70-90% */

/* the whole effect is two variables — swap them per business,
   never per taste:                                              */
--brand-900   shadow colour   // darkest step of the measured ramp
--paper       highlight colour // the page ground, so it disappears into it

Contrast-safety, not just style · pick the shadow/highlight pair for a brightness gap wide enough that text sitting on top still clears 4.5:1 — check the luminance of both stops before shipping, the same as any other text-on-image case in Standards. A duotone that fails contrast is still a duotone that failed contrast.

Where it goes wrong · two colours with a small hue distance (e.g. two blues) reads as a mistake, not a treatment — cross at least 40° of hue, which the brand-900/paper pair already does by construction. And never stack a duotone on top of an image that's also going through GL-09 dithering — pick one, they fight for the same visual role.

KIT 09

Edge language

How things are separated. Small decision, disproportionate effect on perceived cost.

1px hairline
blur shadow
inset highlight
tonal step

Default to the hairline. 1px at 15–20% opacity, 2px radius. 2026 premium reads as engineered precision — sharp geometry and thin rules. The soft blurred shadow with a 16px radius is the most dated thing you can put on a page; it reads as 2019 and it reads as a template.

Use tonal steps — a surface one shade lighter than its ground — where a border would be too much. Use inset highlights only on interactive elements, where the 1px light line along the top edge reads as a real physical control.

KIT 10

Texture & material

What stops large flat areas reading as unfinished — and the layer that makes a coffee shop feel like burlap and scorched wood instead of a generic warm gradient.

Two mechanisms. Everything below is built from these two.

Mesh gradient — several soft radial blobs stacked in one background, each colour drawn from the OKLCH ramp in KIT 01. Real, native CSS — no image, no canvas.

.mesh {
  background:
    radial-gradient(circle at 18% 22%, var(--brand-300) 0%, transparent 55%),
    radial-gradient(circle at 82% 15%, var(--cool-500)  0%, transparent 50%),
    radial-gradient(circle at 60% 78%, var(--brand-700) 0%, transparent 60%),
    var(--paper);
}
// 3-6 blobs. Fewer reads as a plain gradient; more turns muddy.
// Every stop comes from the ramp — never a colour typed by eye.

Grain — an inline SVG feTurbulence filter, encoded once as a data URI, layered as the top background-image. No JS, no canvas, no external file.

Icons take ink, not accent, by default. currentColor inheriting from the surrounding text is correct almost everywhere. Never mix filled and line icons on one page. A filled social-media icon set next to a line-style feature icon set is the icon equivalent of mixing two unrelated type families — it reads as two different people built two different sections.

The tolerances

The floor you don't go below

Ordered by when you hit it: while building, then before shipping.

1 · Demo values are not ship values

Everything in the Effects tab is tuned so the effect is obvious at a glance. Production wants the opposite: the page feels alive and the reader never notices the mechanism. Ship roughly 40–50% of demo values.

If a colleague can describe what the animation did, it's too strong.

The four that most need dialling back: depth travel, velocity skew, particle repulsion, magnet pull. Reveals and staggers were already tuned for production — ship those as-is.

2 · One loop, gated

One requestAnimationFrame for the whole page. Every animated section gated by an IntersectionObserver so nothing off screen computes. A dozen private loops will idle a page at 100% CPU.

Anything added to Effects must register through the shared loop. A technique that ships its own rAF doesn't go in.

3 · Performance

  • Animate transform and opacity only where possible — they're compositor-only. Animating top, width or height forces layout every frame.
  • will-change on anything animating mask-image, clip-path or filter.
  • Separate reads from writes. Batch every getBoundingClientRect before any style write. Interleaving forces synchronous layout each frame — the actual cause of most "why is my scroll animation janky."
  • Passive listeners on scroll, wheel and touchmove.
  • Clamp inputs before mapping, never outputs after.
  • content-visibility: auto on long offscreen sections.

4 · Accessibility

  • prefers-reduced-motion is a designed state, not a kill switch. Turning effects off leaves a dead page. Design the deliberate static composition. This is also the honest answer for vestibular disorders, which large-field parallax genuinely triggers.
  • aria-label on any split text. Splitting a headline into spans destroys the accessible string — label the parent, aria-hidden the pieces.
  • Visible :focus-visible on every control.
  • Real controls. Buttons are <button>. State is announced. A styled div is not a control.
  • Contrast holds during motion — text mid-blur still has to be readable at every frame it's legible in.

5 · Mobile

  • Re-check axis-dependent effects. A horizontal wipe on a one-column phone layout reveals nothing.
  • Pointer effects need a touch story. Tilt, magnetism and cursor-followers do nothing on a phone — provide a device-orientation equivalent or design the static state.
  • Halve particle counts and blur radii. Blur is expensive on mobile GPUs.

6 · Typography

  • Font loading must not shift layout — font-display: swap plus size-adjust and metric overrides on the fallback.
  • Running text near 65 characters; text-wrap: balance on headings.
  • tabular-nums wherever digits line up in columns.

7 · Concept work

  • Label it in the page — visible and persistent.
  • Invent nothing. No prices, reviews, testimonials, credentials or links you haven't verified.
  • Carry the ® on registered marks.
  • Never imply a relationship that doesn't exist.

Before anything ships

  • Ship values, not demo values
  • One rAF loop, all sections gated
  • Reduced-motion has a designed state, not a blank one
  • Split text carries aria-label
  • Every control keyboard-operable with visible focus
  • Mobile: axis effects re-checked, pointer effects have a fallback
  • No layout thrash — reads batched before writes
  • Fonts don't shift layout on load
